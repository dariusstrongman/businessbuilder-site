## 1. The Texas Business Opportunity Act

**Tex. Bus. & Com. Code ch. 51.** The Texas analogue to the federal rule, and it is
more specific about what trips it.

§51.003(a) defines a business opportunity as the sale of services **over $500** used
"to begin a business" where the seller represents **both**:

1. the purchaser "will earn or is likely to earn a profit in excess of the amount of
   the initial consideration the purchaser paid"; **and**
2. the seller will "provide a sales, production, or marketing program".

**Element 2 is satisfied outright.** Business Builder sells lead capture, CRM,
listings, local presence and AI-driven marketing operations. That is a marketing
program by any reading.

**Element 1 is the switch, and it is entirely within Business Builder's control.**
The site currently makes no profit representation and must not start.

If both elements trip:

| Obligation | Provision |
|---|---|
| Disclosure statement filed with the Secretary of State **before advertising or offering** | §51.051 |
| **$25,000** bond, trust account or letter of credit | §51.101 |
| No income representation without "documented data to substantiate" it, **disclosed to the purchaser when the representation is made** | §51.301(3) |
| Any violation is a **per se DTPA violation** | §51.302 |
| "A waiver of this chapter is contrary to public policy and void" | §51.006 |

`[COUNSEL]` Confirm that no profit or earnings representation exists anywhere, in
marketing, in the intake flow, in a sales conversation, or in a scope document. The
obligation attaches **before advertising**, so a determination made after launch is a
determination made too late.

## 2. The federal rule is broader than previously stated

**16 CFR part 437.** The correction matters.

§437.1(c) requires three elements:

1. solicitation into a **new business**
2. a required payment. **There is no dollar floor**, and §437.1(p) reaches
   consideration owed "by practical necessity", which a subscription satisfies
3. a representation that the seller will "**provide outlets, accounts, or customers,
   including … Internet outlets, accounts, or customers, for the purchaser's goods or
   services**"

**No earnings claim is required.** That is the correction to
[04-service-terms-business.md](04-service-terms-business.md), which described the rule
as reaching a payment plus *either* a customer representation *or* an earnings claim.
The customer-provision prong stands alone.

And §437.1(m) extends the third element to "**otherwise assisting the prospective
purchaser in obtaining his or her own … customers**."

That phrase is the problem. Lead capture, local presence, listings, Google Business
Profile setup and an AI worker whose stated job is handling inbound enquiries are all
plausibly assistance in obtaining the purchaser's own customers.

**§437.2 requires the disclosure document at least seven calendar days before the
buyer signs or pays.** That is incompatible with same-day web checkout, which is what
the intake flow is being built toward.

### The enforcement action that should be read in full

**FTC v. Air AI Technologies**, filed in the District of Arizona in August 2025 and
settled **24 March 2026**. An AI company selling to small businesses, charged under
the FTC Act, the Telemarketing Sales Rule **and 16 CFR 437**, over earnings and
refund-guarantee claims. Outcome: an **$18 million judgment**, largely suspended, and
a **permanent ban on marketing business opportunities**.

Three other matters ended the same way: Ascend Ecom, Ecommerce Empire Builders, and
FBA Machine, each with a permanent business-opportunity ban.

`[COUNSEL]` **This is now the most urgent single question in the package.** It affects
whether the offer can be sold on a same-day checkout at all, and the seven-day rule
cannot be satisfied retroactively.

### A related rule with a controllable element

**The Franchise Rule, 16 CFR 436**, needs three elements: trademark association,
significant control or assistance, and a required payment above a low threshold. The
second and third are satisfied. **The first is controllable.** If customers trade
under their own name and never hold out as associated with a Business Builder mark,
there is no franchise. Parts 436 and 437 are mutually exclusive, so the analysis lands
in one or the other.

## 3. The three-day cancellation law, and the pilot

**Tex. Bus. & Com. Code ch. 601.** §601.002 covers consumer transactions **over $25**
where the merchant personally solicited **away from its own place of business** and the
agreement was signed away from it.

**Signing the cleaning company at their own premises triggers this.**

Requirements:

- §601.051: the buyer may cancel "not later than midnight of the third business day"
- §601.052(b)(4): a notice in **boldface type of at least 10 points**, adjacent to the
  signature line, in this exact form:

> YOU, THE BUYER, MAY CANCEL THIS TRANSACTION AT ANY TIME PRIOR TO MIDNIGHT OF THE
> THIRD BUSINESS DAY AFTER THE DATE OF THIS TRANSACTION. SEE THE ATTACHED NOTICE OF
> CANCELLATION FORM FOR AN EXPLANATION OF THIS RIGHT.

- §601.053: a **detachable duplicate** cancellation form
- §601.201: **a non-compliant contract is void**
- §601.204: non-compliance is a per se DTPA violation

§601.002(b)(3)(B) exempts transactions negotiated at a fixed business location.

`[COUNSEL]` The pilot agreement is likely to be signed at the cleaning company's
premises. If it is, the notice and the duplicate form are required and the contract is
**void** without them. Either sign at a fixed business location, or comply. This is
cheap to get right and fatal to get wrong.

## 4. The DTPA, worked out properly

**Tex. Bus. & Com. Code ch. 17.** No amendments since 2019.

### The pilot customer is a covered consumer

§17.45(4) defines a consumer to include a corporation or partnership, excluding only a
business consumer with **assets of $25 million or more**, or one owned by an entity
that size.

**A residential cleaning company is a DTPA consumer. Business-to-business is not an
escape in Texas.**

`[COUNSEL]` Whether an **LLC** qualifies is unresolved. The definition lists
individual, partnership and corporation, and no controlling opinion resolves it. The
research flagged this as unconfirmed.

### A waiver is not available

§17.42(a) sets **one** route with **three cumulative** conditions: written and signed
by the consumer, the consumer **not in a significantly disparate bargaining position**,
and the consumer **represented by legal counsel** in acquiring the services. §17.42(b)
voids it if counsel was identified or suggested by the defendant. §17.42(c) requires
conspicuous boldface of at least 10 points, headed "Waiver of Consumer Rights", in a
prescribed form.

**There is no $100,000 threshold and no alternative route.** A DTPA waiver in a
click-through agreement with an unrepresented small-business customer is void, and
§17.42(e) provides that a signed waiver is not a defence to an action by the Attorney
General anyway.

### And there is no exit at this price point

§17.49(f) exempts a written contract over **$100,000** where the consumer is
represented by counsel. §17.49(g) exempts one over **$500,000** with no counsel
requirement. Neither is inflation-indexed.

At $795, $1,495, $1,995 and $299 a month, **there is no contractual exit from the DTPA
at all.**

### What the exposure actually is

§17.50(b)(1): economic damages. If the conduct was **knowing**, mental anguish plus up
to **three times** economic damages. If **intentional**, up to three times the total.

§17.45(9) defines "knowingly" as actual awareness, "but actual awareness may be
inferred where objective manifestations indicate that a person acted with actual
awareness."

**§17.50(d) makes attorney's fees mandatory and one-way.** A prevailing consumer
"shall be awarded" costs and reasonable fees. Defence fees are available only on a
groundless or bad-faith finding.

§17.50(g) disapplies the general exemplary damages caps. §17.565 sets a two-year
limitation, extendable 180 days for concealment.

### The two mechanisms that actually control this

Because the exposure cannot be contracted away, the controls are procedural.

**§17.506(d)** is a complete bar if, within **30 days** of the §17.505 notice, the
defendant tenders the claimed damages plus reasonably incurred fees.

**§17.5052** settlement offers cap recovery where the tender equals or exceeds the
factfinder's award, and cut off fees from the offer date.

**§17.505(a)** requires written notice at least **60 days before filing**. The remedy
for non-compliance is **abatement, not dismissal**.

`[COUNSEL]` Build an internal process around §17.506(d) and §17.5052 before the first
customer. They are the only meaningful damage-control mechanisms available, and both
run on short clocks that start when a notice letter arrives.

### What this means for disclaimers and caps

Three cases settle it.

***Southwestern Bell Telephone Co. v. FDP Corp.***, 811 S.W.2d 572, 576 (Tex. 1991):
"a liability limitation would be invalid under §17.42 insofar as it purported to waive
liability for an act defined as deceptive under §17.46(b)." **But** because warranty
claims derive from other law, a cap that is genuinely part of the warranty given **does**
limit a DTPA breach-of-warranty claim.

***Prudential Insurance Co. v. Jefferson Associates***, 896 S.W.2d 156, 169 (Tex.
1995): an express DTPA waiver is void, but an "as is" clause is not. It works only
"[w]here the 'as is' clause is an important part of the basis of the bargain, **not an
incidental or 'boiler-plate' provision**, and is entered into by parties of relatively
equal bargaining position." And it collapses entirely if the seller made the
representation that induced it: "**A seller cannot have it both ways.**"

***Cate v. Dover Corp.***, 790 S.W.2d 559 (Tex. 1990): a warranty disclaimer "must be
conspicuous to a reasonable person". Text "undistinguished in typeface, size or color
within a form purporting to grant a warranty is not conspicuous, and is unenforceable."

### The provision that punishes overreaching drafting

**§17.46(b)(12)** makes it a violation to represent "that an agreement confers or
involves rights, remedies, or obligations which it does not have or involve, **or which
are prohibited by law**."

**An unenforceable clause is itself a potential violation.** That reframes the whole
drafting exercise: a term that overreaches is not merely useless, it is affirmatively
risky. It is the strongest argument in this package for the position taken in
[17-liability-warranty-framework.md](17-liability-warranty-framework.md), that a
narrow clause which holds is worth more than a broad one that does not.

`[COUNSEL]` Every disclaimer needs genuinely contrasting typeface, size or colour. A
non-reliance clause only works if negotiated and non-boilerplate. Neither is achievable
in a standard click-through without deliberate design.

## 5. Texas privacy, resolved

**Tex. Bus. & Com. Code ch. 541.**

### Applicability, and the exemption almost certainly applies

§541.002(a) requires all three of: conducting business in Texas or serving Texas
residents; processing or selling personal data; and **not being a small business as
defined by the U.S. Small Business Administration**.

**There is no consumer-count threshold**, unlike several other state statutes.

The SBA standard is a NAICS table with no single number. The relevant rows:

| NAICS | Industry | Standard |
|---|---|---|
| 541511 / 541512 / 541519 | Custom programming, systems design | **$34.0M** |
| 541611 | Administrative and general management consulting | **$24.5M** |
| 541613 | Marketing consulting | **$19.0M** |
| 561499 | All other business support services | **$21.5M** |

Receipts are averaged over five fiscal years and **affiliate receipts are added**.

**Business Builder is exempt for the foreseeable future.**

### The one obligation that survives the exemption

**§541.107**, in full: a small business "may not engage in the sale of personal data
that is sensitive data without receiving prior consent from the consumer", and a
violation carries the full **$7,500 per violation** penalty.

"Sensitive data" includes health, genetic and biometric data processed to uniquely
identify a person, data from a known child, and **precise geolocation**. "Sale" reaches
transfers for "monetary **or other valuable consideration**", so barter and data-swap
arrangements count.

### The cure period is permanent

`[COUNSEL]` answered. **§541.154's 30-day cure period does not sunset.** The words
"expire" and "sunset" appear nowhere in the enforcement subchapter, and the enacting
bill used an express sunset elsewhere, which shows the drafters used explicit language
when they meant it. Texas differs from Connecticut and Colorado here.

Enforcement is Attorney General exclusive. **§541.156: no private right of action.**
§541.155: up to $7,500 per violation after the cure period.

### The exact required notices

Where applicable, §541.102(b) and (c) prescribe these sentences verbatim, including
punctuation:

> NOTICE: We may sell your sensitive personal data.

> NOTICE: We may sell your biometric personal data.

Each must be "posted in the same location and in the same manner as the privacy
notice".

Neither should ever be needed, because Business Builder should not sell either.

### Processor contract terms

§541.104(b) requires six items: clear processing instructions; the nature and purpose;
the type of data; the duration; the rights and obligations of both parties; and a
requirement that the processor ensure confidentiality duties, delete or return data at
the controller's direction, make compliance information available, allow reasonable
assessments, and **engage any subcontractor under a written contract imposing the same
requirements**.

`[BUILD]` The subprocessor flow-down obligation is a contract requirement, not only a
list. See [16-data-retention-deletion.md](16-data-retention-deletion.md).

The 2025 AI statute amended only §541.104(a)(2), adding a duty to assist on the
security of personal data processed by an AI system. **It added no new required
contract term.**

### Rights and deadlines, for the privacy policy

§541.102(a) requires the notice to state the categories processed including sensitive
data, the purpose, **how a consumer appeals a decision**, categories shared, categories
of third parties, and the submission methods. §541.055 requires **two or more secure
and reliable methods**, though an exclusively online controller with a direct
relationship may provide only an email address.

§541.052(b): respond within **45 days**, extendable once by 45 with notice and reason.
§541.053: a conspicuous appeal process, with a written response within **60 days**, and
on denial a link to the Attorney General complaint mechanism.

**§541.054 voids any contract term waiving these rights.**

## 6. Unauthorised practice of law, with the actual authorities

This section replaces the general treatment in
[04-service-terms-business.md](04-service-terms-business.md) with specifics.

### The safe harbour protects the product, not the conversation

**Tex. Gov't Code §81.101(c)** excludes from the practice of law the "**design,
creation, publication, distribution, display, or sale**", including by means of an
internet web site, of written materials, books, forms, computer software or similar
products, "**if the products clearly and conspicuously state that the products are not
a substitute for the advice of an attorney**."

**Six verbs. "Select", "customise", "apply" and "advise" are not among them.**

§81.101(a) defines the practice of law to include a service rendered out of court,
"including the giving of advice or the rendering of any service requiring the use of
legal skill or knowledge, such as preparing a will, **contract**, or other instrument,
the legal effect of which under the facts and conclusions involved must be carefully
determined."

§81.101(b): the definition "is not exclusive and does not deprive the judicial branch
of the power … to determine whether other services and acts not enumerated may
constitute the practice of law." **You cannot clear yourself by reading the statute.**

The safe harbour arrived in June 1999, six months after a federal court enjoined a
legal-forms product, and the Fifth Circuit vacated that injunction specifically because
the Legislature had acted. **The statute legalised the software. It did not decide the
question.**

### The controlling case

***Unauthorized Practice Committee v. Cortez***, 692 S.W.2d 47 (Tex. 1985). Non-lawyers
completing immigration forms for a fee. A jury found no legal skill was required. The
Supreme Court affirmed an injunction anyway:

> "Although the act of **recording a client's responses** to the questions on the form
> … probably does not require legal skill or knowledge, **the act of determining
> whether the [form] should be filed at all does require special legal skills.** …
> advising a client as to whether to file … **requires a careful determination of legal
> consequences**." … "the undisputed activities of the Cortezes **in selecting and
> preparing** the various immigration forms required legal skill and knowledge."

And on who decides: "the courts are not bound by the jury's determination".

*Cortez* also treated the **advertising** as independent evidence, because claims of
deep experience "could likely mislead a customer to believe there is nowhere else to
seek help."

### Three doctrines that close the obvious workarounds

**A disclaimer does not defeat implied advice.** ***Crain v. UPLC***, 11 S.W.3d 328
(Tex. App. 1999), decided **after** the safe harbour took effect: "**In preparing these
documents, CMCC impliedly advises its clients of their legal rights and entitlement
under the law.**" This is the closest Texas analogue that exists: a non-lawyer company
preparing and filing a statutory instrument with a government office for a fee.

**"Free" does not help.** ***Hexter Title & Abstract Co. v. Grievance Committee***, 179
S.W.2d 946 (Tex. 1944): no separate charge was made, but the service "is advertised as
**a leader to induce prospective customers** … It constitutes a part of the total
service for which the customers pay." **A free operating agreement bundled with every
formation package is the exact fact pattern the court rejected.**

**Form selection is legal work.** ***Palmer***, 438 S.W.2d 374 (Tex. 1969): "**The
selection of the proper legal form affects important legal rights.**" The publishing
half of these older cases would likely come out differently now, but §81.101(c) never
addressed **selection**.

### Applied

| Activity | Side of the line |
|---|---|
| Recording the founder's own answers onto a form and filing it | **Permissible.** The one activity *Cortez* affirmatively blesses |
| **Selecting entity type** | **Practice of law.** *Cortez*, *Palmer* |
| Drafting or customising an operating agreement per customer | **Practice of law.** §81.101(a), *Crain*, *Hexter*. Publishing a disclaimered template is the protected path |
| Explaining which option is better **for this customer** | **Practice of law.** "For me" converts information into advice |

The Secretary of State says the same from the other side: it "cannot help you determine
the best entity type for your particular business needs", and that decision should be
made "in consultation with an attorney and accountant."

### The criminal provision that is actually a marketing offence

**Penal Code §38.122:** a person commits an offence if, with intent to obtain an
economic benefit, the person **holds himself or herself out as a lawyer** without being
licensed and in good standing. **Third-degree felony.**

The exposure is in how the service is described, not only in what it does.

**Correction:** Penal Code §38.123 does **not** reach entity formation. All its prongs
are tethered to personal injury or property damage claims. It is a runner and barratry
statute.

**Chapter 83** is real-property specific. It bars non-lawyers from charging for
preparing an instrument "**affecting title to real property**", with treble damages. It
does not reach certificates of formation, operating agreements, EINs or registered-agent
service. It bites only if real estate is contributed to a new entity.

### Two traps

**The notario statute, Gov't Code §406.017.** Triggered **only if the person is a
notary public**, but then criminalises soliciting or accepting compensation "to obtain
relief of any kind on behalf of another **from any officer, agency, or employee of this
state or the United States**" — which describes taking a fee to obtain a Secretary of
State filing or an IRS number. Class A misdemeanor, felony on a prior. §406.017(f)
makes non-compliance a deceptive trade practice under ch. 17. Translation and **typing**
services are carved out, which is the same scrivener line as *Cortez*.

`[COUNSEL]` If a notary is ever on staff for formation or banking paperwork, this needs
a hard look.

**The S-corp question.** Circular 230 defines practice before the IRS to include
"rendering written advice with respect to any entity, transaction, plan or arrangement",
and limits it to attorneys, CPAs and enrolled agents. **Form SS-4 is expressly exempt
from the preparer identification requirement. Form 2553 is not.**

Advising *whether* to elect S-corporation status sits at the intersection of Texas UPL
**and** federal practice before the IRS. It is **the single highest-risk question in a
standard formation funnel.**

### Two honest gaps

**No Texas appellate decision squarely addresses non-lawyer entity formation or entity
selection.** A search across all Texas state courts pairing the unauthorised practice of
law with certificate of formation, articles of incorporation and operating agreement
returned zero on-point results. Everything above is analogy. The analogies are strong
and consistent, and none of them is the answer.

**The Texas LegalZoom matter appears not to exist.** Five independent checks came back
negative, including the company's own sworn filings, which name its California,
Missouri, Alabama, Ohio, Arkansas, South Carolina and North Carolina matters
individually and mention Texas only as an office lease. **Do not build anything on the
idea that a Texas regulator blessed that model.** The documented matter is North
Carolina.

**And Texas has no document-preparer licensing lane.** No analogue to California's legal
document assistant scheme, Arizona's certified preparers or Nevada's. **In Texas the
question is binary, and §81.101(c) is the only statutory shelter.**

## 7. Telephone and SMS, which is why the pilot should have neither

### Texas extended its registration regime to text messages in 2025

**S.B. 140, effective 1 September 2025**, amended §302.001(7) so that "telephone
solicitation" means "a call or other transmission, **including a transmission of a text
or graphic message or of an image**".

§302.101(a): a seller may not solicit from a location in Texas "**or to a purchaser
located in this state**" without a registration certificate. §302.106 sets a **$200**
fee. §302.107 requires **$10,000** of security.

**§302.153 requires filing "all sales information and literature, including scripts,
outlines, instructions".** §302.102(b) makes the filing public information.

**AI system prompts are filing-disclosable under that provision.** That is a startling
consequence and it deserves its own decision.

§302.302 sets a civil penalty up to **$5,000 per violation**. §302.251 makes certain
conduct a Class A misdemeanor. §302.303 makes a violation a per se DTPA violation.
§302.004: "An attempted waiver of a provision of this chapter is void."

The exemptions are narrow. The former-customer exemption requires two years under the
same name. The vendor exemption requires **three years** of continuous operation, which
a pre-launch company cannot satisfy.

### And a federal violation is now a Texas violation

**New §305.054**, also from S.B. 140: a violation of that chapter is a false, misleading
or deceptive act under the DTPA, and DTPA remedies may be used to enforce it. Because
§305.053 already covers communications violating the federal statute, **a federal
telephone-consumer violation is now also a per se Texas DTPA violation**, which is where
the mandatory attorney's fees come from.

**New §305.055:** prior recoveries by the same claimant "may not limit recovery in a
future legal proceeding in any manner."

### Federal position, current

- **AI voice counts as an artificial voice.** In force since February 2024. Prior
  express consent is required, and **prior express written consent** for telemarketing
  content. The order states that the statute "does not allow for any carve out of
  technologies that purport to provide the equivalent of a live agent."
- **The proposed AI-call disclosure rule was never adopted.** It remains a proposed rule.
  There is no federal AI-disclosure requirement for calls.
- **The one-to-one consent rule was vacated** and removed from the regulations in August
  2025.
- **Revocation rules have been binding since April 2025**: revocation by any reasonable
  method, a list of per se revocation words, honoured within **ten business days**, and
  a caller **may not designate an exclusive means** to revoke. The cross-campaign
  propagation piece has been extended to **31 January 2027**, so it is coming.
- Damages are $500 per violation, trebling discretionary, **no federal attorney's fees**
  — which is why the Texas hook matters.
- A text message is a "call".

### Who is liable when an AI worker sends the message

Under the federal definitions **the customer is the "seller" and the platform is the
"telemarketer"**. A person "initiates" a call when it takes the steps necessary to
physically place it, so **the platform is directly liable**. A seller can be vicariously
liable on a broad range of agency principles, and a seller giving "specific and
comprehensive instructions" can be a **direct** initiator — which describes a customer
who writes the prompt and supplies the list.

Meanwhile the Texas provision attaches liability to "the person who **originates** the
communication", which is the platform, with no vicarious-liability provision to redirect
it.

**A single non-consented AI text to a Texas mobile number can simultaneously be a
federal violation, a ch. 305 violation, a ch. 302 violation and a DTPA claim.**

### Design consequences

- Consent must name **the customer** as seller and cannot travel between customers
- The caller bears the burden of proving consent per number
- Revocation must propagate platform-wide and **cannot** be funnelled to a single channel
- Build cross-campaign propagation now, since the extension expires January 2027
- One safe harbour worth knowing: the Texas no-call chapter expressly permits displaying
  "the name and telephone number of the person on whose behalf the call is made", which
  covers showing the customer's caller identification

`[COUNSEL]` The recommendation in
[07-pilot-service-agreement.md](07-pilot-service-agreement.md) to permit **no SMS and no
voice in the pilot** is strongly supported. It removes the registration question, the
bond, the script-filing question, the consent question and the four-way liability
stack, all at once.

## 8. The Texas AI statute, and what it does not do

**HB 149, effective 1 January 2026**, created four chapters, not one.

**Scope is jurisdictionally broad and substantively narrow.** It reaches anyone
promoting or conducting business in Texas, but defines "consumer" as an individual
acting "**only** in an individual or household context", expressly excluding a person
acting in a **commercial or employment** context.

**It does not reach a company using AI to operate a customer's business**, for practical
purposes.

Three things it notably does **not** do:

- **No general AI disclosure duty.** The disclosure obligation binds **governmental
  agencies** and health care providers. **Texas has no analogue to California's bot
  disclosure law.**
- **No impact assessment, no risk assessment, no registration.** All were in the
  introduced bill and were stripped. Commentary describing a Texas AI impact-assessment
  mandate is describing the introduced bill, not the law.
- **No private right of action.** Attorney General exclusive, with a 60-day cure period.

Private-sector prohibitions are all intent-gated, and the statute provides that "**a
disparate impact is not sufficient by itself to demonstrate an intent to discriminate**".

### Two defensive provisions worth designing around

- A **rebuttable presumption that a person used reasonable care**
- A **statutory safe harbour for substantial compliance with the NIST AI Risk Management
  Framework Generative AI Profile**

`[COUNSEL]` The second is concrete and actionable. Aligning the operating-limits and
evidence architecture to that framework buys a statutory safe harbour, and much of the
`[BUILD]` list in [22-security-and-build-gaps.md](22-security-and-build-gaps.md) is
already pointed that way.

### The Texas AI-adjacent law that does bite

**The biometric statute, §503.001.** A person must inform and obtain consent before
capturing a "retina or iris scan, fingerprint, **voiceprint**, or record of hand or face
geometry" for a commercial purpose. **$25,000 per violation.** Attorney General only.

The 2025 amendment exempts training and processing for AI development **unless a system
is used or deployed for the purpose of uniquely identifying a specific individual**.

**A voice AI receptionist that identifies callers is squarely in scope.**

This statute produced the two largest state privacy recoveries in the country, at $1.4
billion and $1.375 billion.

`[COUNSEL]` A further reason for no voice in the pilot, and a hard constraint on any
future voice product.

## 9. Texas has no auto-renewal statute

Confirmed two independent ways. No chapter of the Business & Commerce Code addresses
automatic renewal, and the "six months or more, notice not before the 90th day" language
circulating in secondary sources is **failed bill text** from 2009 and 2011.

**What applies instead is the DTPA**, which reaches business customers under the $25
million asset threshold, with mandatory one-way fees.

**The binding design constraints are the federal statute and the California
auto-renewal law, not Texas law.** See
[24-market-practice-benchmarks.md](24-market-practice-benchmarks.md), section 6.

And a point that settles the question raised there: the Amazon subscription matter,
which produced **$1 billion in civil penalties plus $1.5 billion in redress** in
September 2025, was obtained under the federal statute and the general deception
provision **after the click-to-cancel rule was vacated**. The vacatur changed the source
of the obligation, not the obligation.

The recodified rule now in the regulations is the 1973 prenotification regime for
book-club style plans. **It does not reach a month-to-month service.**

## 10. FTC position on AI claims, which moved both ways

**Weakened.** The order against a writing-tool company was **reopened and set aside by
the Commission itself in December 2025**, on the basis that the complaint's facts failed
to support the allegations and the order "unduly burdens artificial intelligence
innovation". The Bureau director stated: "**Condemning a technology or service simply
because it potentially could be used in a problematic manner is inconsistent with the
law.**" Capability-based liability is substantially weakened federally.

**Strengthened.** An August 2025 order bars effectiveness representations about an AI
product "unless it is not misleading, and the company has **competent and reliable
evidence to support the claim at the time it is made**", with substantiation retention
and four years of compliance reports. And a set of August 2026 orders addressed a
company claiming "a special algorithm to listen in on and detect pertinent conversations
from smart devices" when the service was not based on voice data at all. **That is the
cleanest current authority for "do not claim a capability the product lacks", and it was
a business-to-business case.**

Civil penalties are **$53,088 per violation**.

**One caution:** the 2023 FTC business-blog posts on AI claims now return errors and are
absent from the archives. Do not cite them as current guidance.
