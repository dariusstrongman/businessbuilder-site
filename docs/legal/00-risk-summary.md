# 0. Commercial and legal risk summary

> **NOT LEGAL ADVICE. NOT ATTORNEY REVIEWED.**
> The only document here that assumes no prior context. Read it first.

## The position in three sentences

Business Builder has built a product with an unusually honest public posture and no
contract behind it. The site makes many customer-favourable promises, has no terms
to balance them, and states three things that are not true. Research also turned up
a tax obligation that changes the pricing, not just the paperwork.

Two further rules reach the offer itself, and both attach before the sale rather than
after it, so neither can be repaired later.

None of this is unusual for a company at this stage. All of it has to be closed before
a real customer pays.

---

## The seven risks that matter

### 1. The site is the contract, and it only has one side

**Likelihood: certain. Impact: high.**

There is no Terms of Service, no Privacy Policy, no refund policy and no
cancellation policy. There is no acceptance mechanism and no record of what any
customer agreed to.

Meanwhile the site promises, among other things: research and a scope before any
charge, that nothing built is locked to Business Builder, that the customer owns
everything built, seven separate unqualified "stop any time" statements, no ongoing
fee ever, hosting with no stated term, and that terms do not change after payment.

Every one of those is enforceable against Business Builder. Nothing is enforceable
the other way. In a dispute or a chargeback, the published site is the evidence and
it reads entirely in the customer's favour.

**Mitigation:** documents 01 through 09, plus the contract mechanics in document 22.

### 2. The tax position changes the pricing

**Likelihood: high. Impact: high, and immediate.**

Texas treats software subscriptions as taxable data processing services, with a 20%
exemption leaving 80% taxable. Activation fees follow the service they activate.
Website creation and hosting are taxable on the same basis. Residential cleaning,
which the pilot customer performs, is taxable at the full rate with no exemption.

A Texas business has no threshold to cross before this attaches. The permit is
needed before the first sale.

The published prices say nothing about tax. Either it is added and disclosed before
commitment, or it is absorbed and the real margin is lower than the model assumes.

There is also a direct collision between the intellectual property strategy and the
tax treatment of the website export, which cannot be resolved by drafting alone.

**Mitigation:** document 19, with a Texas CPA.

### 3. Three statements on the site are untrue

**Likelihood: certain, because it is already happening. Impact: high.**

The intake form tells every submitter their build is open and they will be contacted.
Nothing is saved and nothing is sent.

The login page collects an email address and a password and posts to a route with no
handler.

The bundle price is understated by $1,200 on the homepage, the pricing page and the
intake form.

The first of these is the clearest deceptive-practices exposure in the audit, and it
is the one that has already affected real people.

**Mitigation:** document 20, tier 0 and tier 1. The fixes are engineering work, not
legal work.

### 4. The operating limits are described but not enforced

**Likelihood: medium. Impact: high, and uninsured.**

The site makes eighteen distinct assertions about what AI workers can do, must ask
about, and cannot do. These are excellent copy and the right way to sell the
product. They are also a specification.

An instruction to a model not to do something is not a control. If a boundary exists
only as prompt text, the assertion is not currently true.

This compounds with two other findings. The approval boundary is contradicted by the
worker day rendered on the same page. And there is no confirmed insurance position,
so if a worker acts outside its stated limits there may be nothing behind the
liability.

**Mitigation:** documents 14 and 17, and tier 3 of document 22.

### 5. The pilot is the highest-risk engagement and it is first

**Likelihood: medium. Impact: potentially severe.**

Every other engagement is Business Builder doing work for a customer. The pilot is
Business Builder operating a live business serving members of the public in their
homes.

Specific exposures: a booking error reaches somebody's home. End-customer home
addresses combined with scheduled absences are a sensitive combination. Any direction
of workers risks a joint-employment argument. A quote that omits sales tax creates a
liability for the pilot customer. And a liability cap measured by a nominal pilot fee
is worth nothing against harm of that kind.

**Mitigation:** document 07, and it should be the last thing signed rather than the
first.

### 6. Two business-opportunity rules, and neither can be fixed later

**Likelihood: unknown. Impact: high. Urgency: highest in the package.**

A federal rule reaches a service that solicits someone into a **new business**, takes a
required payment with **no dollar floor**, and represents that it will provide customers
for them, including "otherwise assisting the purchaser in obtaining his or her own
customers". **No earnings claim is required.** Lead capture, listings, local presence
and an AI worker whose job is handling enquiries all sit close to that line.

Where it applies, the disclosure must be delivered **seven calendar days before the
buyer signs or pays**, which is incompatible with same-day checkout.

A Texas statute reaches a sale over $500 used to begin a business where the seller
represents **both** a likely profit **and** a marketing program. The second element is
satisfied outright. The first is entirely controllable and the site currently does not
trip it. If both trip: a filing with the Secretary of State before advertising, a
$25,000 bond, and per se deceptive-practices liability.

An enforcement action settled in March 2026 against an AI company selling to small
businesses ended in an $18 million judgment and a **permanent ban on marketing business
opportunities**.

**Mitigation:** [04-service-terms-business.md](04-service-terms-business.md) and
[25-texas-consumer-privacy-upl.md](25-texas-consumer-privacy-upl.md), sections 1 and 2.

### 7. The deceptive practices statute cannot be contracted around

**Likelihood: certain. Impact: shapes every other document.**

Three findings, and together they remove options that a standard technology contract
assumes.

The pilot customer **is** a consumer. The exclusion applies only to a business with
assets of $25 million or more. Business-to-business is not an escape.

**A waiver is not available.** The single route requires the consumer to be
**represented by legal counsel**, with counsel not suggested by the provider. The
large-transaction exemptions sit at $100,000 and $500,000, against offers of $795 to
$1,995.

**Attorney's fees are mandatory and one-way**, treble damages are available on a
finding of knowing conduct, and "knowing" may be inferred from objective
manifestations.

And one provision reframes the drafting exercise entirely: it is a violation to
represent that an agreement confers rights or obligations "which it does not have or
involve, **or which are prohibited by law**." **An unenforceable clause is itself a
potential violation.**

**Mitigation:** [17-liability-warranty-framework.md](17-liability-warranty-framework.md).

### Two that deserve a note

**Risk 10, unbounded pre-payment work.** The site promises research, a
recommendation and a scope before any charge, and says the customer owes nothing if
they walk. That is a deliberate and good commercial choice. It also means Business
Builder performs substantial unpaid work on every enquiry with no cap and no
recovery. At low volume this is a cost of acquisition. At higher volume it is a
business model problem. Worth watching rather than changing.

**Risk 12, apparent authority.** When an AI worker sends a quote from the customer's
own email address, a recipient may reasonably believe it binds the customer. A clause
saying workers have no authority to bind protects Business Builder against the
customer, and does nothing about the third party. This is the most
under-appreciated exposure in the model and it is a product question as much as a
drafting one.

## What is already right

The list above is long. The underlying position is better than it reads, and three
things are genuinely hard to retrofit that have already been done.

**No fabricated proof anywhere.** No testimonials, no borrowed logos, no invented
metrics, no case studies, no countdowns, no crossed-out prices. Actively disclaimed
in five places. The design studies are labelled in five layers and the labelling is
adequate.

**The regulated-activity boundary is already articulated**, in the right vocabulary,
in the product's own content. The failures found are places the copy drifts from a
policy that already exists.

**The third-party fee model is the right design.** The customer buys everything
directly on their own account with no markup and no pass-through. That was chosen
for ownership reasons. It turns out to keep those amounts out of Business Builder's
taxable base as well.

**The ownership and retention boundary is substantially correct** where it is
written. It needs to appear in more than one place and to be worked out at four
edges, not invented.

Most of this package is about making the contract match the posture the site already
takes, rather than about walking anything back. That is a much better starting point
than the reverse.

## What to do this week

Four things, none of which need a lawyer.

1. Fix or remove the intake form, the login page, and the bundle price.
2. Publish the legal entity name, an address and a contact method.
3. Book a Texas CPA for the tax determination.
4. Book a broker conversation about errors and omissions and cyber cover.
5. Decide where the pilot agreement gets signed. Signing at the customer's own premises
   triggers a three-day cancellation statute whose notice requirements, if missed, make
   the contract **void**. Signing at a fixed business location avoids it entirely.

Then document 21 for counsel, and document 23 for the sequence.

## What this package is not

It is not legal advice, it has not been reviewed by an attorney, and no document in
it is ready to publish. Several documents contain open questions only counsel can
close, and several contain sentences that are not yet true and therefore cannot be
published at all regardless of what counsel says.

The goal was to make the business commercially coherent and ready for legal review.
That is what it is.
